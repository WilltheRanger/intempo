How InTempo works, explained without the jargon.

InTempo is a practice app for string players: violin, viola, cello and double bass. You photograph a page of sheet music and play it. The app then tells you whether you rushed (got faster), dragged (got slower) or held the tempo, and exactly where.

Section 1

App overview and core concept

A metronome only clicks at you. It can't tell you that you sped up in bar 9. InTempo works like a patient teacher holding a stopwatch: it listens to one take and points to the bars where your timing drifted.

📷

Photograph the music

Take a picture of the printed page. The app reads the notes off the photo, so it knows what you should be playing and when.

🎻

Play it

Pick a target speed (for example 92 beats per minute), then press record and play. The phone's microphone records the take.

📊

Get a verdict

The server compares when you played each note with when it was supposed to happen, and answers in one plain sentence plus a bar-by-bar chart.

One-line analogy for your video: "InTempo is a karaoke score for classical musicians. Instead of rating whether you hit the notes, it rates whether you hit them on time."

Words you'll hear (mini glossary)

Keep this list on screen if a viewer might not know a term.

Tempo / BPM
The speed of the music, measured in beats per minute. 60 BPM is one beat per second.
Rushing / dragging
Playing faster than the target (rushing) or slower (dragging). Very common, and very hard to hear in your own playing.
Onset
The instant a note starts, the moment the bow bites the string. The app hunts for these in the audio.
OCR / OMR
"Optical character / music recognition": software that reads a picture and turns it into data the computer understands.
Frontend (the app)
Everything you see and touch on the phone: screens, buttons, charts.
Backend (the server)
A computer in the cloud that does the heavy work, like reading music and analysing audio, and keeps your data.
API
The menu of requests the app can send to the server, such as "save this piece" or "analyse this recording".
Database
An organised filing cabinet on the server that holds your pieces, takes and results.

Section 2

High-level architecture: how the pieces fit together

InTempo has two halves that talk over the internet: the app in your hand and the server in the cloud. They share one storage service for accounts, data and files.

Restaurant analogy: the app is the dining room where you sit and order. The API is the waiter who carries orders back and forth. The services are the kitchen where the real cooking happens. Supabase is the pantry and filing cabinet. The AI music readers are specialist chefs the kitchen calls on for one dish.

Section 3

The user journey, step by step

This is what happens from the moment someone opens the app to the moment they read their verdict. Each step names the app screen, the server address, and any storage or AI involved.

  1. 1

    Sign in

    The musician signs in with email. Supabase handles the password or magic link and hands the app a digital ID badge (a "token"). The app shows that badge on every request from then on.

    screens/auth/AuthScreenSupabase AuthGET /v1/me
  2. 2

    Land on "Today"

    The home tab shows the piece you're working on and a big button to practise it. The bottom bar holds four tabs: Today, Library, Insights, Profile.

    screens/today/TodayScreenGET /v1/scores/current
  3. 3

    Add a piece by photographing it

    The camera screen captures one or more pages. Each photo is uploaded to storage first, and then the server is asked to create the piece.

    screens/scanner, capturedPagesPOST /v1/upload/score-imagePOST /v1/scoresphoto stored
  4. 4

    The server reads the music

    A chain of music readers takes turns. The first one is sure enough, it wins. If one fails, the next one tries. The result is a tidy list of bars and notes.

    screens/transcribehomr → Claude → GeminiPOST /v1/scores/{id}/transcribe
  5. 5

    Check and fix the reading

    No reader is perfect, so the musician proof-reads the result and can correct any bar: its notes, clef, key or tempo marking. Then they accept it.

    transcriptionReview, proofRead, measureEditPATCH /v1/scores/{id}POST …/accept
  6. 6

    Pick a tempo and record

    Choose a target speed with a slider. A metronome can count you in. Press record and play; the microphone captures the take as an audio file.

    screens/tempo, screens/recordlib/audio/
  7. 7

    Send the take off to be judged

    The recording is uploaded. The app then asks the server to analyse it. The server replies straight away with "got it, working on it" (code 202 Accepted) rather than making the phone wait.

    POST /v1/upload/audioPOST /v1/analysesaudio stored
  8. 8

    The audio engine does its work

    In the background, the server finds every note start in the recording, lines them up with the notes on the page, and measures how early or late each one was.

    workers/analysis_runnerservices/analysis.py
  9. 9

    Read the verdict

    While it waits, the app asks "done yet?" every few seconds (this is called polling). When the answer arrives, it shows one sentence ("You rushed bars 9–12…"), a coloured bar chart and the annotated score.

    screens/verdict/VerdictScreenGET /v1/analyses/{id}
  10. 10

    See progress over time

    The Insights tab collects every take so the musician can watch a tricky passage improve across the week.

    screens/insightsGET /v1/analyses

Server · security guard

Checking your ID badge

When you sign in, Supabase gives the app a JWT, a digitally signed ID badge. On every request, this file checks the badge is real before letting anything else happen.

  • The badge is signed with a secret key only Supabase holds. The server downloads the matching public key to check the signature, a bit like checking a hologram on a passport.
  • The comment points out a classic trap: forget to check who the badge was issued for ("audience") and every valid user gets rejected.
  • Because of this check, one user can never see another user's pieces or recordings.
Say: "No badge, no entry. And the badge can't be forged, because only Supabase can sign it."

Server · reading sheet music

A relay team of music readers

Turning a photo into notes is hard, so the server doesn't rely on one reader. It lines several up, like a relay team:

  • homr is a specialised sheet-music recognition model. Claude and Gemini are general AI models that can also read a photographed page.
  • Each reader returns a confidence score. At 70% (0.7) or above, the answer is taken right away.
  • A shaky answer is kept as a backup while the next reader has a go. A shaky answer the musician can fix beats no answer at all.
  • Only if every reader fails does the app say "we couldn't read this page".
Say: "If the first expert isn't sure, we ask a second opinion, then a third."

Server · the kitchen manager

Deciding which kitchen cooks the order

Analysing audio needs a lot of memory. The small server has 512 MB in total, and one analysis can use about 460 MB of that.

  • Two analyses at once on the small server could crash it, and take sign-in down with it.
  • So the job can run in one of two places: inprocess (on the server itself, one at a time) or modal (sent to Modal, a service that rents out extra machines on demand).
  • A single setting decides which. The code that takes the order never needs to know.
Say: "When the small kitchen is full, the order goes to a bigger kitchen down the road."

Timing brain · the heart of the app

How a recording becomes a verdict

This is the most important function in the project. The docstring at the top lists the recipe:

load→clean up→find note starts→predict where they should be→line them up→measure→colour→verdict
  • Load and pre-emphasis: open the audio and boost the crisp "attack" sounds so note starts stand out.
  • Onset detect: find every moment a new note begins.
  • Expected onsets: from the sheet music and target BPM, work out exactly when each note should start.
  • DTW (dynamic time warping): a clever way to pair up "what was played" with "what was written", even if you skipped or repeated something. Like lining up two rulers that stretch differently.
  • Graceful failure: if the take can't be trusted (silence, wrong piece), it says so politely instead of inventing a verdict.
Say: "It's a spot-the-difference game between the page and your performance, measured in milliseconds."
backend/app/services/classification.pyPython
def classify_band(delta_pct: float, *, config=None) -> Band:
    """Classify a signed per-beat % deviation into
    a tolerance band.

    Thresholds are asymmetric: rushing and dragging
    have independent inner/mid/outer cutoffs (humans
    tolerate dragging more)."""
    tol = (config or load_audio_config()).tolerance
    magnitude = abs(delta_pct)
    if delta_pct < 0:  # rushing / ahead
        inner, mid, outer = tol.rushing_inner_pct, …
    else:              # dragging / behind
        inner, mid, outer = tol.dragging_inner_pct, …
    if magnitude <= inner: return Band.on
    if magnitude <= mid:   return Band.slight
    if magnitude <= outer: return Band.rush_drag
    return Band.severe
Timing brain · traffic lights

Turning "how late" into a colour

For each note, the app knows how far off it was as a percentage of a beat. This function sorts that number into one of four traffic-light bands:

≤ 5%
On time
5–10%
Slight
10–20%
Rush / drag
> 20%
Severe
  • A negative number means the note came early (rushing). A positive one means it came late (dragging).
  • Rushing and dragging get separate rules, because listeners forgive slightly late notes more than early ones.
  • The cut-off numbers are not in the code. They come from config.toml, so they can be tuned by ear without a programmer.
Say: "Every note gets a traffic light, and those lights become the chart you saw earlier."

Timing brain · the sentence you read

Writing a verdict a human understands

Hundreds of note measurements are useless to a musician on their own. This function boils them down to one sentence.

  • It looks for the longest stretch of notes that all drifted the same way. That's the real problem, not one stray note.
  • It speaks in BPM ("104 against your 92") rather than percentages, because that's how musicians think.
  • The stretch has to be long enough that it isn't just random wobble, so the app doesn't nag you about noise.
Say: "The code speaks the musician's language, not the computer's."

Timing brain · tuning knobs

A mixing desk, not hard-coded numbers

Rather than burying "5%" deep in the code, every sensitivity setting lives in this one file, like faders on a mixing desk.

  • sr = 22050: the audio is sampled 22,050 times a second. Plenty for timing, and quicker to process.
  • [onset.double_bass]: the double bass is very low and rumbly, so it gets its own settings. There's also a filter that ignores sound below 80 Hz.
  • [tolerance]: the traffic-light cut-offs from the previous block.
  • The project's own rule: every change here must be written up in TUNING_LOG.md and re-tested against the saved recordings.
Say: "Changing how strict the app is means editing a number, not rewriting code."

App · the style rulebook

One paint box for the whole app

Colours are stored once, with names like bg or textPrimary, instead of scattered codes like #F7F2E9. These named values are called design tokens.

  • Change a token once and every screen updates together, so nothing ends up "almost the same beige".
  • There's a light and a dark palette. The app follows the phone's setting.
  • Numbers like 8.51:1 are contrast ratios: proof the text is easy to read, even for people with weaker eyesight. Automated tests check them.
  • This guide borrows the same ivory and near-black colours.

Data · the filing cabinet

Building the database one step at a time

The database holds tables: users, scores (pieces), analyses (takes and verdicts) and so on. Nobody builds it all at once. It grows through numbered migrations.

  • Each file is one renovation: "add a table for score pages", "remember which bar the take started from".
  • The numbers force a strict order, so any empty database can be rebuilt exactly by applying 001 → 027.
  • RLS ("row-level security", file 002) is a database rule that means you can only ever see your own rows.
  • A CI check applies every migration to a blank database on each change, to catch broken SQL early.
Say: "The database has a paper trail. Every change to its shape is a numbered file."

Quality · the inspectors

How the project stops mistakes shipping

Every time code is pushed to GitHub, a robot (CI, "continuous integration") runs a checklist. If anything fails, the change isn't merged.

  • Tests are little programs that check other programs. For example: "feed in this saved recording and the verdict must say 'rushed'."
  • Fixtures (saved sample recordings and scores) mean the tests never pay for or wait on a real AI service.
  • preflight runs the same checklist on the developer's own computer first. It's faster than waiting for the robot.
Say: "Hundreds of automatic checks run before any change reaches users."
Honest status (worth saying on camera): the backend pipeline (reading music, analysing audio, the API) is built and heavily tested, and the app's screens are built. Recording has been confirmed on a real iPhone. Checking the full live loop (record → upload → analyse → verdict against the deployed server) end to end, and fine-tuning the timing thresholds against real recordings by ear, are still in progress.

Presenting tips for beginners

  • Explain the "why" before the "how". Viewers follow code far better once they know what problem it solves.
  • You don't need to read every line. Point at the one or two lines the plain-English box mentions.
  • Reuse the analogies (restaurant, relay team, traffic lights). Repetition is what makes them stick.
  • Say "I don't know" honestly. If a viewer asks something off-script, point them to docs/subsystems.md and the code comments. This project documents why things are the way they are.
  • Keep scenes under 90 seconds. Attention drops fast on screen recordings.
